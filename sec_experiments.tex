\section{Computational Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

The experiments answer three questions, and the order matters because
the first is what licenses the other two.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Does the reformulation find good plans where the truth is
  known?} The volumetric problem of Appendix~\ref{appendix:A} is the
  same transport and inventory problem with a volumetric truck
  capacity, and the compact formulation of it is solvable on instances
  of the size this study reaches. It is therefore a genuine yardstick,
  and Section~\ref{sec:res-vol} sets the reformulation against it.
\item \textbf{Does the method keep working on the 3D problem, where no
  reference optimum is available?} The compact 3D model returns no
  incumbent beyond five periods, so it cannot serve as a yardstick;
  Section~\ref{sec:res-3d} reports what the method returns and what it
  can certify, and Section~\ref{sec:res-case} states what remains to
  be measured against the procedure the company uses today.
\item \textbf{Does it matter?} Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction} puts
  the loads a volumetric model prescribes to an independent
  placeability proof, which is the test of whether the 3D
  representation buys anything at all.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Protocol and declared budgets}
\label{sec:protocol}

All calculations use Python. The restricted master, the aggregate
integer master and the partitions of Section~\ref{sec:relaxfix-flow},
the volumetric benchmark and the compact models are solved with Gurobi
12; the pricing problems
\eqref{bp:PP-objective}--\eqref{bp:PP-s-stack-w} and the placeability
tests with CP-SAT from OR-Tools 9.12. Every run reported in
Tables~\ref{tab:volumetric}--\ref{tab:fleetfloor} is read from a
recorded run file, named in a comment beside the row in the source of
the table.

\paragraph{Every row carries its configuration, or says that it does not.}
Five parameters can change the answer a solver returns: the wall-clock
\emph{time limit}; the relative \emph{MIP gap} at which an integer
solve may stop; the \emph{thread count}; the \emph{memory cap} per
model; and the random \emph{seed}. Table~\ref{tab:protocol} states them
per stage. They are read from each run's own record and not from the
script that launched it --- with one exception, which is marked
wherever it appears. The volumetric fleet-tree runs of
Table~\ref{tab:volumetric} recorded no configuration, and theirs is
\emph{recovered} after the fact from their launcher scripts and the
system journal; one of those five runs has no recoverable time limit at
all. A recovered configuration is weaker evidence than a recorded one
and is never silently mixed with it.

\paragraph{Memory is a budget, not an accident.}
Gurobi holds its branch-and-bound tree in memory and imposes no limit
of its own, so a compact model whose dual bound has stalled simply
grows its list of unexplored nodes until the machine intervenes. Every
model is therefore given an explicit per-model soft memory limit, at
which the solver stops and returns its incumbent and its dual bound
with the status \textsc{mem\_limit}, exactly as a time limit would. Two
consequences follow. A solve that stopped on memory is a solve that
stopped, and its dual bound is a valid lower bound on the model's
optimum regardless of why it stopped. And reaching a \emph{fixed}
per-model cap sooner on a larger model is a statement about the cap and
not about tractability: no inference is drawn here from \emph{when} the
cap was reached, and the cap is reported as a parameter of the
experiment like the time limit.

\paragraph{What each reported quantity means.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $z$ is the cost \eqref{eq:MPObjective} of the plan returned, net
  of any artificial penalty; a plan is reported only if it carries no
  artificial slack. For the fleet tree it is the incumbent of
  Algorithm~\ref{alg:bp3d}; for the compact models it is the solver's
  incumbent at its stop. It is an upper bound on the optimum in every
  case.
\item \emph{trucks} is $\sum_{g,t}n_{gt}$ for that plan.
\item $\mathrm{LB}$ is a lower bound on the optimum of the model the
  method solves. For the fleet tree it is the \emph{certified} bound of
  Section~\ref{sec:bounds}: the root certificate, or the minimum over
  the certified bounds of the open frontier when that is higher. A
  bound is printed only where the sweep behind it came back with every
  pair proved; where it did not, the row says \emph{unproved} and no
  gap is computed. For a compact model it is the solver's dual bound at
  its stop. A sentinel recorded by a run that obtained no bound is
  never printed, never averaged and never rounded to a side.
\item \emph{gap} is $(z-\mathrm{LB})/z$ against the method's own
  bound. It is an optimality gap only to the extent that the bound is
  tight, and Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction} identifies exactly where
  it is not. It is never a gap to a proven optimum unless the row says
  so.
\item \emph{stop} names the condition under which a solve ended:
  \textsc{optimal}, \textsc{time\_limit} or \textsc{mem\_limit}. A time
  or memory stop makes the row's time the budget rather than a solve
  time.
\item \emph{nodes} is the tree nodes opened and \emph{open} the regions
  left unexpanded when time ran out; a run with open regions has not
  searched the space, whatever its gap.
\item \emph{verified} is the outcome of re-deriving every dispatched
  truck of a plan from the column's own fill and checking it against
  \eqref{eq:col-geom}--\eqref{eq:col-agg} --- placement, stack height,
  crush limit, payload and the utilisation floor --- by a routine that
  shares no code with the solver that produced it.
  \emph{unplaceable} counts, among the loads a \emph{volumetric} plan
  dispatches, those a constraint programme has \emph{proved} cannot be
  stacked and placed in a trailer; loads it could not decide within its
  limit are reported as \emph{undecided} alongside, and are counted
  neither way.
\item $\Delta z$ is the cost of one method relative to another on the
  same instance. The denominator is stated wherever it appears, and
  $\Delta z$ is meaningful only between plans both of which are
  executable.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Instance generation}
\label{sec:instances}

Thirty-six benchmark instances are generated as a full factorial over
the four explanatory variables of the study: the number of products
$|N|\in\{4,8\}$, the horizon $|T|\in\{5,10\}$, the number of RTI types
$|R|\in\{2,3,4\}$ and the volumetric granularity
$v\in\{60,90,120\}$. Instances are named $I\_|N|\_|T|\_|R|\_v$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Container dimensions}: footprints are drawn from the
  industrial catalogue $\{1200\times800,\;1200\times1000,\;
  800\times600,\;1200\times1600,\;2400\times1200\}$ mm. Heights are
  quantised to $50$ mm and a footprint is admissible for a given $v$
  only if the implied erected height lies in $[400,1500]$ mm, so that
  an RTI is worth stacking and at least two layers fit under the
  trailer roof. Whenever $|R|\ge3$ several RTI types share a
  footprint, reproducing a real catalogue in which container families
  sit on a common pallet base; this is what allows a stack to mix
  types across layers.

  \item \textbf{Container volume}: $v$ is the number of erected RTIs
  that fill one truck by volume, and every RTI type is generated with
  volume at least $\Gamma_{K}/v$. Larger $v$ means smaller RTIs and a
  harder placement subproblem; smaller $v$ means a single container is
  a larger fraction of a trailer.

  \item \textbf{Demand}: generated per product and period by deviating
  $\pm10\%$ from a mean calibrated so that roughly $2.5$ truckloads
  are consumed per period. Calibrating the mean keeps difficulty
  comparable across the factorial instead of letting it drift with the
  RTI size.

  \item \textbf{Weights}: the content weight of a full RTI scales with
  its volume and varies by $\pm25\%$ between products sharing an RTI
  type, which is what makes the per-stack crush limit depend on the
  period's product mix rather than on the RTI type alone.
\end{itemize}

The truck is a standard semi-trailer,
$pK\times qK\times rK=13600\times2400\times3000$ mm, payload
$pK^{w}=24$ t, per-stack crush limit $W^{\max}=1200$ kg, minimum
utilisation $\alpha=0.55$ and cost $cK=1000$ per dispatch. Lead time
is $lt=1$ and coverage stock one period of demand.

\paragraph{A size ladder.}
The benchmark tops out at $|N|=8$ and $|T|=10$. To find where, if
anywhere, the decomposition pays against the compact model, a second
set of instances is generated with the same rules along two axes:
$(|N|,|T|)\in\{(8,10),(12,12),(16,15),(24,20)\}$, and the number of
truckloads consumed per period, $2.5$, $5$ or $8$, which scales the
fleet $K$ and with it the number of interchangeable trucks the compact
model indexes. The suffix is that figure times ten and is \emph{not}
$K$: $I\_8\_10\_2\_90\_k25$, $I\_12\_12\_2\_90\_k25$ and
$I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ have $K=12$, and $I\_24\_20\_2\_90\_k50$ has
$K=21$. These instances have $|R|=2$ and $v=90$. They stress different
things: $|N|$ and $|T|$ grow the master and the number of pricing
calls, while the fleet grows the compact model's per-truck variables
and its symmetry.

\paragraph{Sizing the RTI fleet.}
One generation parameter deserves comment because it is easy to get
wrong and the symptom is misleading. Empties return by the truckload,
and a return truck leaves only once the plant has accumulated enough
folded RTIs to clear the full-truckload floor. Since folded RTIs pack
very densely, that takes several periods, during which the supplier
must live off its own folded stock. If the fleet is sized only for the
lead time, the linear relaxation remains feasible by dispatching
fractional trucks while the integer problem has no solution at all. We
therefore size the supplier's initial folded stock of each type to
cover $lt+SC$ periods plus the number of periods, capped at six,
needed to accumulate $\lceil\alpha\Gamma_{K}/\gamma_{sr}\rceil$ folded
RTIs at the plant's rate of emptying them, which is the standard
closed-loop argument that an RTI fleet must cover the cycle time.

\subsection{Methods compared, and which procedure produced which table}
\label{sec:methods}

Three solution procedures appear in the recorded results of this study,
and only the first is the method of Section~\ref{sec:math}. They are
listed with the tables they produced, because a table labelled with the
method's name but produced by another procedure is the single easiest
way for a study like this one to mislead.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MATH-3D-SCPTOP} --- Algorithm~\ref{alg:bp3d} exactly as
      Section~\ref{sec:math} states it: the master of
      Section~\ref{sec:mp} over a starting pool of the placeable
      single-commodity trucks and nothing else, columns from the
      pricing ladder of Section~\ref{sec:pp}, the flow-first relax and
      fix of Section~\ref{sec:relaxfix-flow} as primal heuristic with
      the partition run as a cover, every part proved placeable before
      it becomes a column, dominance cuts on the parts proved
      unplaceable, integer stack counts in the aggregate master, the
      region $X\le X^{\star}-1$ constructed first, and the bound from
      the volumetric relaxation
      (Proposition~\ref{prop:inclusion}). Every dispatched truck is
      verified. \emph{This produced Tables~\ref{tab:bench},
      \ref{tab:summary} and
      \ref{tab:3d-onecommit}--\ref{tab:fleetfloor}, and every
      MATH-3D-SCPTOP number quoted in this paper.}
\item \textbf{The same method over the volumetric oracle} --- the same
      master, the same search and the same construction, with the
      pricing problem replaced by the two-constraint knapsack of
      Section~\ref{sec:pp}, so that a column is a volumetrically
      feasible full truck. \emph{This produced the fleet-tree columns
      of Table~\ref{tab:volumetric}.}
\item \textbf{An earlier branch-and-price configuration}, in which the
      primal heuristic is the integer master over a pool seeded with
      the volumetric benchmark's placeable loads rather than the
      flow-first construction. It is a different procedure --- a
      different pool, a different primal, and no placeability cuts ---
      and on $I\_8\_5\_2\_90$ it returns $20\,767.81$ with fourteen trucks
      where MATH-3D-SCPTOP returns $18\,796.96$ with twelve.
      \emph{No table in this paper reports it.} Its recorded output,
      \texttt{results/benchmark.csv} and
      \texttt{results/case\_study.csv}, is not used for any method
      column here; the volumetric half of the first of those files,
      which involves no branch and price at all, is used for the
      placeability census of Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction} and for
      nothing else.
\end{itemize}

The comparators are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Compact SCPTOP} --- the volumetric model of
      Appendix~\ref{appendix:A} solved directly by Gurobi. It solves
      the same problem as the fleet tree over the volumetric oracle, so
      the two bracket a common optimum, and it is the one comparator in
      this study that produces a \emph{proved} optimum.
\item \textbf{Compact 3D-SCPTOP} --- the model of
      Section~\ref{sec:model} solved directly at $|L|=3$ loading
      configurations per direction, warm-started with the
      matheuristic's plan. $|L|=3$ is a restriction chosen a priori:
      nothing here computes an over-estimate of $|L|$, and
      Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} states the equivalence only for an
      unrestricted $|L|$. Its \emph{plan} is therefore a legitimate
      comparator and its dual bound is not an end of any interval.
\item \textbf{SCPTOP} --- the volumetric benchmark, identical to the
      master in every respect \emph{except} that truck capacity is
      volumetric. It is not used as a yardstick for the 3D method; its
      role is Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction}, where the loads it
      dispatches are put to a placeability proof.
\item \textbf{Company procedure (PLC-SCPTOP)} --- the master over a
      fixed pool of manually derived loading configurations, as
      produced today by a spreadsheet. This is the intended comparator
      for the 3D rows, and Section~\ref{sec:res-case} states why no
      number is reported against it here.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The volumetric model against the compact SCPTOP formulation}
\label{sec:res-vol}

\input{tab_volumetric}

Table~\ref{tab:volumetric} is the comparison in which the truth is
sometimes known. On the volumetric problem the compact formulation is
tractable at the sizes this study reaches --- it closes two of the six
instances to proved optimality --- so the reformulation can be measured
against a reference rather than against itself.

\paragraph{Against a proved optimum.}
On $I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ the compact model proves the optimum,
$68\,139.17$ with forty-five trucks, in $1\,349$ s of a $3\,600$ s
budget at a $20$ GB cap. The fleet tree over the volumetric oracle
returns $68\,139.59$, also with forty-five trucks: $0.0006\%$ above a
\emph{proved} optimum, and at the same fleet. Its own certified bound on
the same instance, $68\,131.43$, lies $0.0114\%$ below that optimum, so
both ends of the reformulation's interval are within about a hundredth
of a percent of the truth. This is the strongest credibility datum in
the paper, and the reason for reporting it first is that nothing
elsewhere in the study can be checked this way.

Three qualifications belong with it rather than after it, and the third
is the one that limits what it proves.

The budgets are \emph{not} equal, and the inequality runs against the
fleet tree: the compact model had twice the time and five times the
memory. The fleet-tree run recorded no configuration of its own, so the
$1\,800$ s and the $4$ GB in the table are recovered from its launcher
and the system journal, with no seed and no commit established; a
recovered configuration is weaker than a recorded one, and what it
supports is the objective comparison --- two numbers on one instance ---
and not a timing claim. And the five volumetric runs are five days older
than the 3D campaign: they ran the same \emph{scheme} --- the master of
Section~\ref{sec:mp}, the pure single-commodity pool, the flow-first
relax and fix, the two-level fleet branch and bound --- but an earlier
revision of it, in which step 3 solved an exact split rather than the
cover of Section~\ref{sec:relaxfix-flow}, step 2 carried no integer
stack counts, and level 1 did not recurse. They are therefore evidence
about the scheme and not a measurement of the code that produced
Table~\ref{tab:3d-onecommit}. Re-running them on the current code is a
cheap and obvious next step, and until it is done this row is the
weakest-provenanced of the paper's strong claims.

One thing the volumetric rows do measure cleanly is the tree, because
there it certifies per node. On $I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ its root
construction returns $68\,151.00$ at $332$ s and four node improvements
take that to $68\,139.59$, a gain of $0.0167\%$ that lands within
$0.0006\%$ of the proved optimum. The pattern repeats on the three
larger rows: root plans of $133\,506.22$, $200\,053.56$ and
$281\,024.98$ improved by three, five and two node improvements to
$133\,503.74$, $200\,047.54$ and $281\,023.77$ --- gains of $0.0019\%$,
$0.0030\%$ and $0.0004\%$. The tree earns little here and much on the
3D rows, which is consistent with what the root leaves it: where the
relaxation is nearly exact the construction is already there.

\paragraph{Where the compact model stops on memory.}
On the other four instances the compact model exhausts its $20$ GB cap
with its dual bound still short of its incumbent. The fleet tree's plan
is cheaper on all four --- by $0.0058\%$, $0.6276\%$, $0.1092\%$ and
$0.3821\%$ of the compact incumbent --- and uses one truck fewer on two
of them. Its certified bound is the tighter of the two on three of the
four; on $I\_8\_10\_2\_90\_k25$ the compact model's dual bound,
$38\,851.93$, is tighter than the reformulation's $38\,791.14$ by
$0.157\%$. The budget asymmetry means a row on which the fleet tree is
cheaper is not a budget artefact, while the one row on which its bound
is looser may be.

\paragraph{The bound, tighter and sooner.}
The clearest statement about the bound does not need the recovered
runs. On $I\_12\_12\_2\_90\_k25$ the reformulation certifies
$52\,105.25$ after $26.3$ s of its $1\,800$ s 3D run --- a full sweep in
which every $(g,t)$ pair priced optimal and nothing priced out, over
the volumetric relaxation and so valid for the 3D problem by
Proposition~\ref{prop:inclusion}. The compact volumetric model on the
same instance, given $600$ s, ten threads and a $20$ GB cap, stops at a
dual bound of $51\,944.22$ with an incumbent of $52\,116.69$. The
decomposition's bound is $0.310\%$ tighter and arrives in a
twenty-third of the time. The two budgets differ and are stated for
that reason; both bounds are valid bounds on the same relaxation.

\paragraph{The horizon walk, measured once.}
The volumetric problem is also where the second relax and fix of
Section~\ref{sec:relaxfix-walk} has its recorded results, and they are
reported here rather than beside the procedure. Eight runs on
$I\_24\_20\_2\_90\_k50$ at a $600$ s budget, over the volumetric master
and final pools of $551$ to $3\,518$ columns: \emph{every} incumbent any
of the eight produced came from the walk, and none from the closing
integer master over the whole horizon, which is the obstruction the walk
exists to get round. The best was $206\,492.17$ with $137$ trucks
against a certified bound of $200\,013.36$ --- a gap of $3.14\%$ of the
incumbent, on the definition of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Under period
scoping and without the pricing repair, two of the eight returned no
plan at all.

These runs are not comparable with the fleet-tree rows above: a
different budget, a different machine configuration, and a procedure
that was never asked for the same thing. What they establish is the one
structural fact the walk was built for --- that at twenty periods the
monolithic integer master produces nothing and a committed prefix
produces something --- and nothing about which primal is better.

\paragraph{The compact 3D model cannot serve as a yardstick.}
Table~\ref{tab:3d-head} sets the compact 3D-SCPTOP model against the
method on four rungs of the ladder, and the finding is a negative one
that the rest of this section depends on. On $I\_8\_5\_2\_90$ --- the
one instance on which the compact model returns a plan --- it has three
configurations per direction, $67\,674$ variables and $92\,092$
constraints, and stops on its $1\,800$ s time limit at $18\,816.13$ with
a dual bound of $18\,795.76$, no phantom freight, twelve loads that pass
the independent verifier, and a peak resident set of $3.8$ GB against
the $20$ GB cap. The method reaches $18\,796.96$ with twelve verified
trucks at $140.2$ s, a plan $0.102\%$ cheaper, which the $67$ nodes the
tree then opens do not improve.

From ten periods on the compact model returns \emph{no feasible
solution at all}. On $I\_8\_10\_2\_90\_k25$ ($119\,424$ variables,
$164\,692$ constraints at $|L|=3$), on $I\_12\_12\_2\_90\_k25$
($200\,748$ and $256\,180$) and on $I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ ($322\,734$
and $393\,412$) it finds none in $1\,800$ s. All three stopped on time,
not on memory: their peak resident sets were $6.1$, $2.7$ and $4.5$ GB
against the declared $20$ GB cap, so the runs had their memory budget
and did not use it, and the honest statement is that the model could
not reach a first incumbent in the time it was given. Nor is this a
guard rejecting a corrupt solution: the leak counter is zero because
there was no solution to leak. Four rungs are four rungs and we do not
generalise beyond the sizes they cover; what they establish is that
beyond five periods the compact 3D model supplies neither a plan to
compare against nor a bound that bounds the problem --- its dual bound
bounds the $|L|=3$ restriction --- which is why the comparator for the
3D rows has to be the procedure in use rather than the exact model.

\paragraph{One proved optimum, and it is not the method's.}
On $I\_8\_5\_2\_90$ the volumetric relaxation solves to proved
optimality at $18\,796.96$ in $0.18$ s, and the verified 3D plan costs
the same to machine precision. Every placeable load is volumetrically
feasible, so $z^{\star}_{\mathrm{3D}}\ge z^{\star}_{\mathrm{vol}}$, and
a feasible 3D plan attaining the latter is optimal. That is the only
optimum this paper reports, and it is established by inclusion from an
exactly solved relaxation --- no tree, no pricing, no geometric
certification. The method's own attempt at the same proof failed:
certifying, under their own duals, the three regions its search could
not refute returned $18\,795.57$, $18\,795.54$ and $18\,795.54$ against
an incumbent of $18\,796.96$, none of them proved, at $237$ s per
region. The reason is arithmetic rather than accidental --- with the
fleet fixed at twelve trucks, refuting a leaf needs a maximum reduced
cost below roughly $0.12$ per column, while pricing on the same
instance was still returning columns an order of magnitude below that.
The proof is available on this instance because its two container
families have coinciding height-limited and crush-limited layer counts,
which is precisely the condition under which
Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction} finds a volumetric capacity adequate.

\paragraph{The size ladder.}
Table~\ref{tab:3d-onecommit} reports the method on five rungs, every
row on one commit and verified truck by truck. Read as a progression it
says three things, and the gaps have to be read with one convention
fixed: the gap column of that table is to the \emph{method's own}
certified bound on every row, so that no row carries two gaps, and
where the compact volumetric model supplies a tighter valid bound it is
reported in Table~\ref{tab:volumetric} and used as the interval end in
Table~\ref{tab:3d-head}, never as a second gap.

Against its own bound the gap is $0.0076\%$ at five periods,
$0.165\%$ at ten and $0.046\%$ at twelve --- so two of the first three
rungs are under $0.05\%$ and the third is under $0.2\%$ --- and on all
three the truck count is the minimum possible, because the region with
one truck fewer is certified infeasible
(Table~\ref{tab:fleetfloor}): $X\le11$, $X\le25$ and $X\le33$ are
infeasible in $1.5$, $3.9$ and $16.3$ s, and the reported plans use
twelve, twenty-six and thirty-four trucks. What remains of those three
gaps is inventory cost. At fifteen and twenty periods the method still
returns verified plans --- fifty-four trucks at $78\,549.14$ and one
hundred and sixty at $230\,594.36$ --- but the gap jumps to $13.3\%$,
and Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction} attributes that to the bound
rather than to the plan. Over the thirty-six generated instances the
mean of the same gap is $8.8\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:summary}); nothing in
this paper supports a blanket statement that the method's gaps are
small, and the one that can be made is the conditional one: they are
small where the volumetric relaxation is nearly exact, and that
condition is checkable in advance (Section~\ref{sec:res-abstraction}). On
$I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ neither $X\le52$ nor $X\le45$ is refuted, so
nothing about the truck count is proved there, for the same reason the
bound is loose: the volumetric relaxation admits forty-five-truck plans
--- its own optimum has forty-five --- and cannot separate the
fifty-four-truck 3D plan from fifty-three.

\paragraph{Twenty periods: the round cap is what made the plan possible.}
The twenty-period row deserves a note because an earlier revision of
this work reported that instance as unsolved, and the difference is one
parameter. Under an uncapped cut-and-repack walk the construction spent
$2\,224$ s of a $3\,065$ s run inside the partition --- $387$ cut rounds
over the first fourteen of forty (direction, period) pairs, $8\,709$
placeability calls --- and reached the deadline with no plan at all.
Step 3's deliverable is columns and not the partition, which the cover
of Section~\ref{sec:relaxfix-flow} already conceded, so a pair whose
parts will not place need not be pursued round after round: with the
rounds capped at two, the parts that did place kept as columns and the
failing pairs left to step 4, the same instance returns a verified
one-hundred-and-sixty-truck plan at $230\,594.36$, the root
construction finding it at $1\,418$ s. Forty-four of the eighty-seven
(direction, period) pairs the construction attempted were left to step
4 in that run. The cap is a parameter of a primal heuristic, so this is
a statement about the heuristic and not about the problem.

\paragraph{The full factorial.}
Tables~\ref{tab:bench} and~\ref{tab:summary} run the same method over
the thirty-six generated instances at a uniform $900$ s, one commit,
sequentially. It returns a verified plan on thirty-one of thirty-six
and a proved bound on thirty-five; no plan carries a verification
complaint, and every wall time is at or inside the budget. The mean
certified gap over the thirty rows that have both a plan and a proved
bound is $8.8\%$.

The five failures are the informative part, and they are not spread
across the factorial: all five have $v=60$, the coarsest granularity,
where RTIs are largest and a single container is the largest fraction
of a trailer. At $v=60$ the method returns a plan on seven of twelve
instances and its mean gap on those is $19.3\%$; at $v=90$ and $v=120$
it returns a plan on twelve of twelve with mean gaps of $5.0\%$ and
$7.4\%$. The same granularity is where the volumetric benchmark's loads
are least placeable ($91.8\%$ proved unplaceable, against $55.2\%$ and
$66.7\%$), so the two difficulties have a common cause: when one
container is a large fraction of the trailer, the integer structure of
the loading problem is coarse, the volumetric relaxation is at its
least informative, and the construction has the least room to cover a
delivery vector with full trucks. A plan is not returned rather than an
invalid one being returned, which is a consequence of the formulation:
a column that the verifier would reject never enters the pool.

\paragraph{What the tree contributes, and where.}
The contribution is uneven across the ladder and is not averaged away.
At five periods the tree opens $67$ nodes and improves nothing: the
root construction returns $18\,796.96$ at $140.2$ s and that is the
plan. At ten periods it opens $68$ and seven incumbent improvements
take $38\,865.30$ at $135.9$ s to $38\,855.34$ at $590.7$ s, a gain of
$0.026\%$. At twelve periods it opens $11$ and five improvements take
$53\,195.84$ at $459.0$ s to $52\,129.45$ at $1\,648.5$ s, a gain of
$2.005\%$ --- this is where branching does the most work in the paper.
At fifteen and twenty it opens $1$ and $2$ nodes and the reported plan
is the root construction's. Over the thirty-six generated instances the
node counts range from $0$ to $126$; they are not a predictor of the
gap, which $I\_4\_5\_2\_90$ makes plain at $126$ nodes and $10.0\%$.

Repeat runs matter at these margins, and the exclusion is stated rather
than applied. On $I\_12\_12\_2\_90\_k25$ two runs of the same knob set
on two commits returned $52\,129.45$ and $52\,127.89$, both with
thirty-four trucks, a spread of $0.003\%$; two further runs under a
delivery-branching variant since withdrawn for a diagnosed defect
returned $53\,005.78$ and $53\,425.07$, both with thirty-five trucks.
The method is deadline-driven at every phase, so a step-2 solve landing
at $299$ s rather than $301$ s takes a different branch; no objective
delta below a few hundredths of a percent on an instance of this kind
is reported here without its measured spread beside it.

\subsection{Against the procedure in use}
\label{sec:res-case}

The comparator for the 3D rows is the company procedure, because
Section~\ref{sec:res-3d} established that the compact 3D model cannot
serve as one. \textbf{That comparison is not reported in this
revision.} The reason is stated here rather than deferred, because the
file that would fill the table exists and must not be read as filling
it.

\begin{center}\small
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.92\textwidth}
\textsc{Not available in this revision.} The case study comparing
MATH-3D-SCPTOP against PLC-SCPTOP on the nine company instances
requires a run of the method of Section~\ref{sec:math} against
baselines solved to the gap the comparison claims. Neither condition is
met by the recorded run on disk, for two independent reasons given
below, so no cost, truck count or percentage saving is quoted against
the company procedure anywhere in this paper.
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

First, the method column of that run was produced by the earlier
branch-and-price configuration of Section~\ref{sec:methods} and not by
the method of Section~\ref{sec:math} --- a pool seeded with the
company's own configurations, and the integer master rather than the
flow-first relax and fix as the primal. Labelling it with the method's
name would be the error this section is organised to avoid.

Second, and independently, all nine company baselines stopped on their
time limit rather than at the requested MIP gap of zero. The evaluation
solve is given $315$ s of the instance's $900$ s budget and every one of
the nine returned \textsc{time\_limit}; the stage's recorded wall times,
$513.8$ to $1\,493.6$ s, are larger because the stage also rebuilds the
configuration pool in up to four rounds of $180$ s before the evaluation
solve, and three instances ran concurrently. A
saving measured over a baseline that was not solved is a saving over
whatever the baseline's solver happened to hold when the clock ran out;
it bounds the true saving from one side only, and in the wrong
direction for the claim, since a better-solved baseline can only reduce
it. The same run makes the point against itself: its nine savings over
the company procedure span $-1.25\%$ to $+18.47\%$ and are
\emph{negative} on two instances, which is not a result that survives
either defect being repaired in an unknown direction.

Two observations from the same run do \emph{not} depend on the
solution procedure and are reported, because they are properties of the
company's configuration set rather than of anything the method did.
Reconstructing that set from the planner's average mixes yields very few
distinct configurations when demand is stable, and on three of the nine
instances the reconstruction had to be extended by $77$, $66$ and $73$
configurations before the programme could be served at all; on the
other six it needed none. And on eight of the nine instances the
\emph{dedicated} reading of a configuration --- one per product, rather
than shared across an RTI family --- served the programme better than
the shared one. Both say the same thing about the practical cost of a
fixed configuration set: it is not that the procedure fails, since the
plant plainly operates, but that keeping it feasible as the programme
moves requires a planner to maintain a large and growing catalogue by
hand, every entry of which was found by trial rather than by design.

\subsection{Is a volumetric capacity adequate?}
\label{sec:res-abstraction}

This is the question that decides whether the 3D representation buys
anything, and it is answered by proof rather than by argument: every
truck load the volumetric benchmark dispatches is submitted to a
constraint programme asking whether that exact set of RTIs can be
stacked and placed in a trailer at all, with the true slot bound
\eqref{sb:eq:Ibound} in force so that an infeasibility verdict is a
proof and not a modelling artefact.

\paragraph{The census.}
Over the thirty-six generated instances the volumetric benchmark
dispatches $724$ truck loads. Of those, $501$ ($69.2\%$) are
\emph{proved} unplaceable, $49$ ($6.8\%$) are proved placeable, and
$174$ ($24.0\%$) are left undecided within the limit. The undecided
share must be read with the other two, and it is not noise: a
constraint programme cannot decide easy loads quickly either, and the
last column of Table~\ref{tab:bench} reports the count per instance ---
not to be confused with the \emph{und.}\ column under
\emph{partition}, which counts compositions the construction proposed
rather than loads SCPTOP dispatched. What the census
supports is therefore a statement about the loads it \emph{decided}:
of the $550$ loads decided either way, $91.1\%$ cannot be placed.

The error has a direction, and the direction is what matters
operationally. A volumetric capacity ignores the remainders that
footprints and layer heights leave unusable, so where it errs it errs
by dispatching loads that do not fit. A planner handed such a plan
discovers the shortfall on the loading dock, not in the model. By
granularity the shares are $91.8\%$ proved unplaceable at $v=60$ (with
only $2.7\%$ undecided, so that row is nearly fully decided), $55.2\%$
at $v=90$ and $66.7\%$ at $v=120$.

The two ladder instances on which the crush limit binds give the same
finding with no undecided caveat at all, because there the oracle
decided every load. On $I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$, where the volumetric
plan is the \emph{proved} optimum, $41$ of its $45$ dispatched loads are
proved unplaceable and four are placeable, none undecided; on
$I\_24\_20\_2\_90\_k50$ it is $119$ of $137$, again none undecided. A
volumetric optimum can therefore be a plan nine tenths of which cannot
be loaded, and that statement rests on a complete audit rather than on
a share of the decided.

\paragraph{What volumetric planning costs, constructed rather than estimated.}
The census says a volumetric plan's loads frequently cannot be placed;
it does not say what that costs, and the cost is the answer to the third
question of this section. To answer that, each volumetric plan is
repaired into an executable one on the two instances where the crush
limit binds. The \emph{competent} repair keeps the volumetric plan's
delivery quantities unchanged and re-packs each period's freight with
the same partition-and-pack machinery the method itself uses; every
resulting truck is verified. A weaker, load-local repair --- splitting
each unplaceable load where it stands, with no consolidation --- is
reported for contrast and is \emph{not} used as the comparison, because
consolidation is worth twelve and forty-four trucks and a planner would
do it.

\paragraph{A harder volumetric baseline, and what is not yet known about it.}
The comparison above is against a \emph{plain} volumetric model, and the
obvious objection is that a planner who had read this section would not
build one: the mechanism is computable from the catalogue, so the
capacity could be deflated to reflect it. The honest answer is that a
step in that direction has been measured and does not close the gap,
and that the rest of it has not been measured at all. Adding to the
compact volumetric model the stack-count rows of
Section~\ref{sec:geo3d} --- an integer number of stacks per footprint,
each within the crush limit and the height budget, their floor areas
summed against the trailer floor, which is a valid inequality for every
placeable load --- raises its answer on $I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ from
forty-five trucks to forty-eight, at $71\,072.06$ with a dual bound of
$70\,435.04$ after $600$ s, and raises the dual bound of the relaxation
by $2\,303$, about a fifth of the gap the plain bound leaves. On
$I\_12\_12\_2\_90\_k25$, where the crush limit does not bind, the same
rows change the truck count not at all.

Two things follow, and we draw only those. The rows are a real
tightening of the \emph{bound} and are named in the conclusions as the
next step for it. But forty-eight trucks is still eleven percent below
the method's verified fifty-four, and \emph{whether those forty-eight
loads can be placed has not been tested}: the stack rows are necessary
for placeability, not sufficient, since they say nothing about how the
stacks tile the floor. Until that test is run, the stack-aware
volumetric model is not a baseline this paper can report a saving over,
and claiming one would repeat exactly the error the plain volumetric
model is being faulted for.

\paragraph{The mechanism behind all of it.}
Both the census and the cost above have one cause, and it is worth
isolating because it is the part a planner can check in advance. The
error of a volumetric capacity is governed by how much it over-credits a
truck relative to what physically fits, and the clearest single case is
measured directly. On $I\_16\_15\_2\_90\_k25$ the
payload admits $57$ full RTIs of product $P_{1}$ in one truck, and $32$
is the largest number that can be placed --- a proved bound, obtained by
running the pricing problem for that commodity alone to optimality.
The cause is that the family's container is $650$ mm tall, so four
layers fit under the $3\,000$ mm roof, while at $378$ to $484$ kg full
only two fit under the $1\,200$ kg crush limit. A volumetric fill
therefore credits a truck with roughly twice the placeable count of
that family. The same divergence is why the certified bound on the two
crush-binding rungs of Table~\ref{tab:3d-onecommit} sits $13.3\%$ below
the best verified plan: the bound credits every such stack with four
layers where two can be carried, and we attribute the gap to the bound
rather than to the plan on that evidence --- noting that nothing in this
paper proves it, and that the fifty-four- and one-hundred-and-sixty-truck
plans are reported as what they are, verified upper bounds under a
stated budget.

\paragraph{The layer ratio alone does not predict it, and we say so.}
It is tempting to turn the mechanism into a one-number test, and the
obvious candidate fails. The sweep is eighteen generated instances: three seeds crossed with six
dose levels, where the dose is a scaling of the product weights alone,
so that within a seed the footprints, heights and demands are identical
across the six levels and only the weights move. Over those eighteen,
the share of a volumetric plan's own dispatched loads that a
placeability proof rejects correlates at only $0.42$ with the ratio of
height-limited to crush-limited layer counts. Three of the eighteen
volumetric solves stopped on their time limit rather than at
optimality, which affects which plan was audited but not the audit. The sweep
contains three instances at $0\%$ with that ratio between $0.98$ and
$1.25$, and one at $90.9\%$ with the ratio at $1.08$. The ratio is part
of the mechanism and is not a predictor of the share, and a predictor
validated across instances is not established here. What \emph{is}
established is narrower, and it cuts in both directions, so we state it
as it is. Over the fifteen container types of the industrial catalogue
the ratio of volumetric credit to placeable count --- call it $\theta$,
so that $\theta>1$ means a volumetric capacity credits a truck with more
of that type than physically fits --- has median $0.923$, ranges from
$0.316$ to $2.00$, and exceeds $1.20$ on four of the fifteen types.
Read correctly that says two things. The median real type is
\emph{under}-credited by $7.7\%$, so on a catalogue dominated by such
types a volumetric capacity is conservative and the criticism of it does
not apply. And four of fifteen are over-credited by more than a fifth,
which is enough for a plan drawn over a mixed catalogue to contain
unplaceable loads, since a plan fails on its worst container and not on
its median one.

The instances on which the census above was taken are
\emph{generated}, with $\theta$ in a range the generator controls, and
the catalogue measurement is a realism check on that range rather than a
second census. Two statements therefore survive and a third does not.
It survives that a volumetric capacity can err badly and that where it
errs it errs by prescribing loads that cannot be placed --- the census
proves that on the generated set, and the mechanism explains it. It
survives that $\theta$ is computable from the catalogue and the trailer
alone, with no model solved, so a planner can tell in advance which
regime a catalogue is in. It does \emph{not} survive that a volumetric
capacity is always or usually inadequate: on this catalogue most types
say the opposite, and a study that wanted that conclusion would have to
take its census on real catalogues and not on generated ones.

\subsection{What the bounds and the claims rest on}
\label{sec:res-bounds}

Four statements belong with the results rather than with the claims, and
they are made here so that every number in this section is read
correctly.

\paragraph{No optimum is proved by the method.}
Optimality is claimed in this paper exactly once, on
$I\_8\_5\_2\_90$, and it is established by inclusion from an exactly
solved relaxation and not by the method's own certification
(Section~\ref{sec:res-3d}). In every run reported here the global bound
equals the root bound, because a region's certificate is taken from the
volumetric relaxation and no such certificate can exceed the
relaxation's own optimum; where that optimum coincides with the 3D
optimum the method can prove optimality by inclusion, and where the
crush limit binds and it does not, the residual gap is not closable by
more search. Regions remain open on every row of
Table~\ref{tab:3d-onecommit} but the first, and a run with open regions
has not searched the space whatever its gap.

\paragraph{What the bound costs and what the alternatives return.}
Certifying the volumetric relaxation is cheap and is the reason the
division of labour of Section~\ref{sec:bounds} is affordable: over the
forty-one runs reported here the sweep returns in $2.5$ to $111.8$ s and
comes back with every pair proved on forty of them, the exception being
the one row of Table~\ref{tab:bench} that prints \emph{unproved}. The
geometric sweep is the other extreme: it certified no region on any run
in this study, and the three leaves of $I\_8\_5\_2\_90$ that the search
could not refute took $237$ s each to attempt and returned nothing
proved. The Lagrangian bound \eqref{eq:lagr} sits between them and is
reported rather than relied on, for the reason given when it was
stated: on $I\_8\_5\_2\_90$, whose optimum is $18\,796.96$, it returns
$18\,778.31$ --- valid, and beaten by the volumetric certificate's
$18\,795.54$, because ten proved pairs at $|K|=12$ accumulate $120$
units of penalty against a target of $1.42$.

\paragraph{A lower bound is printed only where a sweep proved it.}
Where no full sweep completed with every pair proved, the value the run
records is the restricted master's, which is an \emph{upper} bound on
the master LP, and it is never printed as a bound. One row of
Table~\ref{tab:bench} is in that position and says \emph{unproved}; it
is excluded from the mean gap, and the count of rows behind every mean
gap in Table~\ref{tab:summary} is printed beside it for that reason.

\paragraph{The compact 3D model's bound is not a bound on the problem.}
It restricts each direction a priori to $|L|=3$ loading configurations,
and Proposition~\ref{prop:equiv} states the equivalence only for an
unrestricted $|L|$. A restriction's optimum is at or above the
problem's, so its dual bound bounds that restriction and not
$z^{\star}$. Its \emph{plan} is used as a comparator and its bound
never as an end of an interval. In particular the compact model and the
method do \emph{not} bracket the optimum on $I\_8\_5\_2\_90$; the
interval that holds $z^{\star}$ there is $[18\,796.96,\,18\,796.96]$,
and it comes from the volumetric relaxation.

\paragraph{Every reported plan is verified, and the verifier is independent.}
Every plan in Tables~\ref{tab:bench} and
\ref{tab:3d-onecommit}--\ref{tab:fleetfloor} is re-derived truck by
truck from each column's own fill and checked against
\eqref{eq:col-geom}--\eqref{eq:col-agg} by code that shares nothing
with the solver that produced it, including the utilisation floor; no
plan reported here carries a complaint. That is a property of the
formulation rather than of the search: a column is a witnessed layout,
so a plan assembled from columns is executable by construction and can
be handed to a loading crew as a drawing. One limitation of the
verifier is worth recording: it checks the per-slot crush limit from the
stack weight stored with the column rather than re-deriving it from the
product fill it is handed, so a load whose products were relabelled
after the column was built could pass. Every producer of columns in the
implementation keeps the two consistent, so no reported plan is
affected, but the check is weaker than the rest of the verifier and
making it independent is a correction rather than a research question.
